Negative result · September 2026

Nothing here can rank peptides for a protein it hasn’t seen

A held-out-target benchmark for peptide binding, the controls that make it falsifiable, and the finding that the standard metric rewards a model which ranks nothing at all.

The published leave-target-out result for this task is a Spearman correlation of 0.530. A model that is structurally incapable of ranking peptides scores 0.678 on the same metric.

01The question, and the metric that dodges it

Given one protein and a shortlist of peptides, which peptides bind tightest? That is the question a screen actually asks, because you hold one target and a fixed number of wells.

Published benchmarks answer a different question. They pool every held-out pair into one correlation, which sums two abilities: knowing which proteins bind tightly in general, and knowing which peptides suit a particular protein. Only the second is useful.

In this dataset 44.5% of the affinity variance lies between targets rather than within them. So nearly half the signal is available to a model that never looks at the peptide.

The oracle is handed each held-out target’s own mean affinity. It gives every peptide for that target an identical score, so it cannot order them — and its per-target correlation is exactly zero, as it must be. On the pooled metric it scores 0.678.

Peptide length is the sharper result. One integer per peptide, carrying nothing whatever about the protein, unable even to distinguish two peptides of equal length. It scores 0.320 pooled. The published 0.530 sits between the two.

What this does and does not claim

The oracle is an upper bound, not an achievable score: a deployed model does not know a held-out target’s mean and would have to predict it from the sequence. The claim is about the metric, not about any particular paper’s models.

Held out means the family, not the sequence

Published leave-target-out splits exclude by exact sequence. A two-residue variant of a training target binds much the same peptides, so that is not a holdout. Targets here are clustered by local-alignment identity and the split moves whole clusters: 122 / 30 / 51 targets, and no test–train pair meets the relatedness rule.

Percent identity alone does not separate related proteins from unrelated ones. Random target pairs in this dataset have a median best-local-alignment identity of 0.33, reaching 0.83. What separates them is coverage: those alignments span a median of 8% of the shorter sequence. Requiring 30% identity across 50% coverage calls 0.8% of random pairs related, while an identical sequence and a 20%-mutated copy both pass.

A bug this caught

The first clustering rule used global alignment normalised by the shorter sequence. On targets spanning 72 to 7,073 residues it called 23% of random pairs related and chained all 203 into a single cluster, putting every target in the training split. Measuring the rule against random pairs is what found it.

Every interval crosses zero. No model beats chance, and none is distinguishable from any other. Seeing the protein as well as the peptide adds nothing over seeing only the peptide, and ESM-2’s peptide embedding is not distinguishable from the peptide’s length.

Two structural checks hold exactly

The target-only model scores per-target +0.000, because its prediction cannot vary within a target. And a linear model on concatenated features predicts wₜ·xₜ + wₚ·xₚ, so for a fixed target the first term is a constant offset and the peptide ordering is identical for every protein. Measured across two different targets, the rank correlation between its orderings is 1.000000. The interaction model, which has an xₜ ⊙ xₚ term, gives 0.376.

04Ruling out the obvious objections

It is not the linear probe

Train R² nearly doubles; held-out ranking does not move. The forest fits the training pairs far harder and generalises identically. And the ordering is the finding: the more target information a model is given, the lower its mean and the higher its variance.

A false positive this caught

On seed 42 alone the interaction forest scored +0.110 with a bootstrap interval of [+0.017, +0.209], excluding zero. It would have been reported as the first real signal. Ten seeds put that value near the top of its own noise distribution, and paired against peptide-only it is behind on 7 of 10. Bootstrapping over targets cannot see this, because it resamples targets while holding the fitted model fixed. Only refitting does.

It is not the pooling

Mean-pooling an 888-residue protein into 1,280 numbers averages a binding site — perhaps ten residues — over the whole sequence. One cross-attention block, peptide residues as queries and target residues as keys, removes that step.

It scores highest of anything here — and substituting a different protein’s residues costs 0.013, less than the 0.017 spread between seeds. Whatever it gained is not target information.

The attention weights say why. On an 888-residue target their entropy is 6.743 nats against a uniform maximum of 6.789 — an effective 849 residues attended out of 888. The most-weighted residue receives 0.0056 where uniform gives 0.0011. Different peptides’ attention maps correlate at 0.993, so the weighting is not peptide-specific either.

Given freedom to look anywhere, the model learned to look everywhere equally. Attention did not fail to recover what pooling discarded. Attention re-derived pooling.

It is not the encoder being frozen

Unfreezing ESM-2’s top layers under the same head made it worse, at both an aggressive and a gentle setting.

05Is 122 targets simply too few?

If the signal were present but under-trained, adding targets should grow it. The quantity to watch is not the raw score — most of which comes from the peptide path and does not depend on target count — but the gap between scoring a held-out target with its own residues and with another’s. That difference is target information and nothing else.

Five seeds each. Four times the targets moves the signal by +0.010 against a seed spread of ±0.016, and the trend is not monotone.

This experiment is weaker than it looks

The range is 4×. Scaling effects are typically log-linear, so a requirement of 100× more data would be invisible here. At a standard error near 0.007 per point it could only have detected a signal of about 0.015. It did not rule out a data problem; it failed to find one in a range too narrow to look.

06What the evidence actually supports

It is tempting to conclude that frozen ESM-2 features do not encode peptide–protein binding. The evidence does not support that. It supports something narrower and more useful: this dataset cannot answer the question.

122 targets is very few from which to learn a function over proteins. And the task as constructed is harder than the one anyone cares about. Within a target, peptides are far more similar to one another (median 2-mer overlap 0.200) than peptides drawn from different targets (0.000). 46 of the 203 targets are essentially one lead peptide and its point mutants.

For those, “rank the peptides for this protein” means “predict which single-residue substitution binds tighter” — a ΔΔG problem, and a much harder one than does this peptide bind this protein at all, which is what sequence-based binder retrieval is usually asked to do.

Those are properties of the data, not of the encoder.

What survives regardless

The metric result stands independent of data volume. A peptide-blind oracle scores 0.678 where the published number is 0.530, and a model that ranks nothing cannot be evidence of ranking. That is a fact about how the field reports this task.

07What would move it

  • More targets, not more model. Every architectural direction here is exhausted; the data axis is not. Peptide–MHC binding offers hundreds of thousands of measured pairs with genuinely diverse peptides rather than mutational series — though MHC alleles are homologous, so it buys depth per target and little target diversity.
  • Separate the two tasks. Ranking diverse peptides against a target and ranking point mutants of a lead are different problems and should not share a metric.
  • Report both numbers. Pooled correlation alongside a per-target one, always, with a control that cannot rank.

08Method notes

Metrics. Per-target Spearman, averaged over held-out targets; a prediction constant within a target scores 0 rather than undefined. Top-20% enrichment with shortlist ceil(0.2n), so the random expectation is k/n and not 0.2. Skill is mean absolute error against the target’s own median affinity, a deliberately generous reference that uses labels a deployed model would not have. Every model in this report scores below zero on it.

Calibration. Hit-rate calibration is not possible: PPIKB collects published binders and has no unbiased negative screen, so a probability fitted against synthetic negatives reports the synthetic ratio. Scale calibration — whether a predicted affinity lands on the right part of the scale — is defined without negatives, and is what “skill” measures.

Encoding. ESM-2 650M, mean-pooled over residues rather than the start token, which was never trained to summarise. The 37 targets longer than the 1,022-residue context are chunked and pooled, not truncated.

Pooled Kd, Ki and IC50. As the published benchmarks do. They are not the same quantity, and this ceiling belongs to the dataset rather than to any model.
